TASK 6: CRITICAL EVALUATION OF EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
Hospital Readmission Consultancy

1. CONTEXT

This consultancy uses retrospective observational discharge records. The analysis can identify associations and support risk stratification, but it cannot prove that a discharge intervention causes fewer readmissions. A future experiment should compare standard and enhanced discharge support using random assignment.

2. COMPLETELY RANDOMIZED DESIGN (CRD)

A CRD would randomly assign eligible discharged patients to two groups:

- Intervention: nurse-led follow-up call within 48 hours, medication review, and scheduled primary-care follow-up.
- Control: existing standard discharge process.
- Primary outcome: readmission within 30 days.
- Secondary outcomes: emergency visits, follow-up completion, patient experience, and intervention cost.

Advantages:

1. Randomization balances measured and unmeasured confounding on average.
2. The design is easy to communicate and analyse.
3. A single intervention can be tested with a clear endpoint.
4. The study can estimate absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat.

Limitations:

1. A single hospital or unit may limit generalisability.
2. A single intervention may not be appropriate for every risk level.
3. Patients and staff cannot usually be blinded to a follow-up call.
4. Contamination may occur if staff provide enhanced care to control patients.
5. Withholding enhanced support may raise ethical concerns; control patients must still receive standard of care.

3. RANDOMIZED COMPLETE BLOCK DESIGN (RCBD)

An RCBD would stratify patients before randomization using pre-specified risk groups, for example low, moderate, and high predicted risk. Patients would then be randomized within each block to standard or enhanced support.

Example:

- Low risk: standard discharge versus enhanced discharge checklist.
- Moderate risk: one follow-up call versus two calls plus medication reconciliation.
- High risk: standard case management versus intensive multidisciplinary follow-up.

Advantages:

1. Risk blocks reduce outcome variability and improve statistical efficiency.
2. The design tests whether intervention benefit differs by risk group.
3. It supports resource allocation because intensive care can be evaluated where expected benefit is greatest.
4. It aligns with the proposed HREWS workflow.
5. Block-specific results are directly actionable for hospital managers.

Limitations:

1. Risk cutoffs must be set before outcome analysis and validated externally.
2. Small or imbalanced blocks can reduce statistical power.
3. The analysis must include block effects and treatment-by-block interaction.
4. A risk model may reproduce historical inequities if it is not audited.
5. RCBD is more complex to operate than a simple CRD.

4. RECOMMENDED DESIGN

RCBD is preferred for a readmission-prevention pilot because the central operational question is not simply whether follow-up works. It is whether different levels of follow-up produce enough benefit for different patient-risk groups to justify their resource cost.

The trial should use intention-to-treat analysis, electronic health-record outcome capture where possible, a pre-registered analysis plan, and a sample-size calculation based on a clinically meaningful absolute reduction in 30-day readmission. Attrition and unavailable external readmissions should be considered before recruitment.

5. IMPLEMENTATION RISKS AND MITIGATION

- Loss to follow-up: use EHR, claims, telephone, and primary-care sources where permitted.
- Protocol non-adherence: use staff training, checklists, audit logs, and weekly feedback.
- Contamination: randomize at an appropriate unit or time level if patient-level allocation is impractical.
- Ethical concerns: compare standard of care with enhanced care and define stopping rules.
- Model error: validate the risk model on current hospital data before blocking.
- Equity: report recruitment, treatment receipt, and outcomes by race, gender, age, and other relevant groups.

6. CONCLUSION

CRD offers a simple causal test of one intervention. RCBD is more suitable for this consultancy because it links experimental evidence to risk-stratified clinical operations. The current observational findings should therefore be treated as a basis for designing a pilot, not as proof that HREWS will reduce readmissions.
