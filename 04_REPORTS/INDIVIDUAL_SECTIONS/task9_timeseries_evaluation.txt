TASK 9: TIME SERIES ANALYSIS EVALUATION
Hospital Readmission Consultancy

1. CURRENT DATA LIMITATION

The available record is an encounter-level dataset with a single readmission outcome for each encounter. It does not provide a complete, time-ordered sequence of admissions, readmission dates, daily observations, or intervention dates. Traditional patient-level ARIMA or exponential-smoothing analysis is therefore not appropriate for the current table.

2. FUTURE SCENARIO A: PATIENT LONGITUDINAL DATA

If encounters could be linked over time, the project could analyse days to first readmission using survival analysis, repeated readmissions using recurrent-event or frailty models, and transitions between stable, at-risk, readmitted, and recovered states using Markov models. This would support identification of frequent users and personalized follow-up timing.

Required data include a reliable patient identifier, admission and discharge timestamps, complete readmission capture, and censoring information.

3. FUTURE SCENARIO B: HOSPITAL AGGREGATE DATA

Monthly admissions and 30-day readmission counts could form a hospital-level time series. Suitable methods include binomial or beta-binomial rate models, seasonal decomposition, exponential smoothing, ARIMA, and interrupted time-series analysis when a policy change has a known implementation date.

This is the most feasible immediate extension. It could forecast workload, identify seasonal patterns, monitor quality, and evaluate changes in discharge policy. Counts and denominators should be retained; modelling rates alone can produce misleading uncertainty.

4. FUTURE SCENARIO C: CLINICAL TRAJECTORY DATA

Daily or hourly vital signs, laboratory results, medication changes, and functional measures during an admission would permit trajectory analysis. Mixed-effects models, functional data analysis, landmark prediction, or recurrent neural networks could assess whether the direction and stability of recovery predicts safe discharge.

This requires standardized measurement, careful handling of irregular timing and missingness, and prospective validation. It should not be implemented solely because a method is technically available.

5. RECOMMENDATION

Start with monthly hospital-level aggregation if dates and denominators are available. Next, develop a prospective clinical-trajectory dataset. Patient-level recurrent-event analysis should follow only after complete longitudinal linkage is established.

6. CONCLUSION

The present data support cross-sectional risk modelling, not traditional patient-level time-series modelling. Time-series methods are promising future tools, but their validity depends on collecting the temporal structure needed by the proposed question.
